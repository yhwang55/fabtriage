When a chip fab alarm fires, the agent finds out why and proves it.

FabTriage investigates film-thickness and sensor alarms in a simulated CVD deposition area, proposes what to hold and what to release with cited evidence, and then brings a held chamber back to target. Built on Meta's Muse Spark.

Step 1

Alarm

LOT-1239 reads 516.70 Å on DEP01-B, above the 515 Å spec limit. Something has to be decided: hold it or let it go.

Step 2

Investigation

Muse Spark picks its own checks: the lot's route, all four chambers side by side, the PM log, the thickness trend, a commonality test, the heater sensors. Ten tool calls, every result numbered E1 to E10.

Step 3

Verdict

Hold the lot and the chamber: DEP01-B came back from PM about 15 Å thick. Every claim cites its evidence, and plain code checks every number before an engineer sees it.

Step 4

Recovery

The held chamber is brought back: two monitor wafers, a recipe 2.9% shorter, and 40 production lots inside the limits. Keeping the old recipe would have sent 11 of them off target.

It picks its checks for the alarm in front of it

Each row is one kind of alarm, ten alarms each. A bigger dot means the agent used that check on more of them. For a suspected metrology misread it asks for a re-measurement; for a sensor alarm it goes to the sensors and the film trend and skips the commonality test. Almost no two alarms got the same path. The fixed playbook, in the last row, runs the same checks in the same order every time.

What the verifier catches

With the verifier switched off, the agent still made the right call on all 70 alarms. But 19 of its 70 dispositions did not carry the evidence the playbook asks for, so an engineer could not check them at a glance. With the verifier on, those are sent back and the agent goes and gets the evidence: none slip through.

Held a chamber without citing a comparison

Before holding a chamber, the playbook wants evidence that it differs from the others: a side-by-side summary or a commonality test, cited in the disposition.

One of them (Chamber after PM): “DEP01-A came back from PM at 68.75 running thick, with both lots after PM over control limits.” The conclusion was right; the evidence the playbook requires was not cited.

Released a one-off wafer without the chamber's history

Releasing a wafer as noise needs the chamber's trend or summary, cited, to show nothing else moved.

One of them (One-off wafer): “Single wafer on DEP01-B reads thick on both metrology tools, but the lot right before it on the same chamber and clean tool was normal and other clean lots were normal with no FDC excursion.” The conclusion was right; the evidence the playbook requires was not cited.

What 100 alarms would cost

Scaled from the 70-alarm set under stated assumptions. Holding everything is safe but costs about 250 engineer hours and a needless hold on more than half the alarms. The fixed playbook and the agent take the same review time, about 17 engineer hours to read and sign off 100 dispositions, but the playbook lets real faults through: about 23 lots at risk. The agent had no needless holds and no missed faults on this set.

Assumptions: 2.5 for engineer hours per manual investigation, 0.17 for engineer hours per review, 2.5 for lot hours per needless hold, 2.0 for lots at risk per missed fault. Practitioner input and our own estimates; see the README.

Where it breaks

Faults keep their size while the evidence gets weaker: noisier film, fewer measured lots, faults that began more recently. The agent holds up through level 2. At level 3 its lead is gone, but it fails safe: no missed faults, more needless holds.

A simulated fab, not production data. Scenarios follow public fab case studies and practitioner input. The recovery idea comes from Prof. Jang (The Catholic University of Korea). Built by Yoon Hwang for the Meta Global AI Developer Hackathon.
